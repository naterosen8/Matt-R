A Field Guide

Gardening across California's eight climates

No other state packs this much climate variety into one border — foggy redwood coast, Mediterranean bay shoreline, scorching desert, and alpine mountain, sometimes within a two-hour drive of each other. This is a region-by-region breakdown of what actually grows well, and why.

The Root Cause

Why one state has this much climate diversity

Three things do almost all the work: the cold Pacific current and its fog, the mountain ranges that block or funnel that moisture, and elevation. Follow a straight line east from the ocean and you cross more distinct growing climates than most countries have.

Moist ocean air cools and drops fog along the coast; the Coast Range wrings out more of it before it reaches the Central Valley, which bakes in summer with no marine relief. The Sierra Nevada then blocks nearly everything else, casting a rain shadow over the desert on the far side — the driest, hottest ground in the state, just a few hundred miles from the foggiest.

Applies Everywhere

Water-wise gardening, statewide

Nearly every California climate shares one trait: little to no summer rain. These practices pay off no matter which region you're in.

Hydrozone your beds

Group plants by water need, not just looks. Thirsty vegetables in one zone, drought-tolerant natives in another — it stops you from overwatering the plants that don't need it just to keep the ones that do alive.

Mulch, always

A 2–3 inch layer of organic mulch cuts evaporation dramatically and moderates soil temperature — the single highest-leverage, lowest-cost thing you can do in every region on this page.

Drip over spray

Drip irrigation puts water at the root zone instead of the air, cutting losses to evaporation and wind drift — especially important in the deserts and Central Valley, where summer air is bone-dry.

Go native where you can

California native plants evolved for exactly this dry-summer pattern. Working them into non-edible parts of the landscape can cut outdoor water use substantially without giving up a productive vegetable garden.

Right plant, right microclimate

A few miles — or a few hundred feet of elevation — can shift you a full zone. Observe your own yard's frost pockets, wind exposure, and sun before trusting a region-wide average too literally.

Build the soil, not just the plant

Compost improves water retention in sandy desert soil and drainage in valley clay alike — it's the rare fix that helps at both extremes of California's soil spectrum.
